EV Battery Cost Trends and Energy Storage Economics

Battery prices are falling again, but the headline average conceals a more consequential shift: chemistry, factory utilisation and supply-chain design now determine who captures the benefit.

01 · Battery market overview

The pack price tells only half the story

Battery costs resumed their decline in 2026 as raw-material prices eased, factories lifted utilisation and cell designs became simpler to assemble. GreenScope’s volume-weighted estimate places the average electric-vehicle pack at $92 per kilowatt-hour. Yet no single buyer pays the average: chemistry, format, production region and contract timing create a wide range of outcomes.

For automakers, lower costs create room to reduce vehicle prices, extend driving range or protect margins. For stationary storage developers, abundant lithium iron phosphate cells are improving project economics and making longer-duration configurations more practical in markets with volatile hourly electricity prices.

02 · Cost evolution

Learning curves meet factory reality

Cell design improvements remain important, but manufacturing execution has become the decisive variable. Plants operating near design capacity spread depreciation, energy and labour costs across more units. Underused facilities face the reverse effect, which explains why pack quotations can diverge even when producers buy similar materials.

Pack integration is another source of savings. Cell-to-pack structures remove intermediate modules, reduce inactive material and simplify assembly. The trade-off is more demanding thermal control and repair planning, particularly for large packs expected to remain in service for well over a decade.

03 · Chemistry comparison

Chemistry follows the use case

LFP has become the default choice for cost-sensitive vehicles and stationary storage because it combines durable cycle life, strong thermal stability and reduced exposure to nickel and cobalt. Nickel-rich chemistries retain an advantage where mass and volume are constrained, especially in premium and long-range vehicles.

04 · Manufacturing scale

Utilisation separates capacity from output

Announced manufacturing capacity comfortably exceeds near-term demand, but nameplate figures overstate what the industry can deliver economically. Qualification cycles, yield improvement and workforce training delay full production. The strongest plants combine high automation with stable energy supply and proximity to cathode, anode and separator production.

Regionalisation continues as automakers seek supply assurance and policy eligibility. Local production can shorten logistics and improve traceability, although smaller regional plants must overcome higher equipment and input costs. Partnerships that guarantee demand during ramp-up are proving more durable than speculative factories without committed buyers.

05 · Supply chain

Materials shift from scarcity to resilience

Lithium availability has improved after several years of investment, moving attention toward conversion quality, graphite processing and the resilience of midstream supply. Buyers are broadening supplier portfolios and placing greater weight on energy use, water management and provenance.

  • Lithium: a more balanced market reduces spot-price pressure, while qualification remains slow.
  • Graphite: processing concentration encourages investment in synthetic and alternative sources.
  • Nickel: abundant supply supports costs, but quality and emissions vary widely between routes.
  • Logistics: regional cell production reduces shipping exposure but does not eliminate upstream concentration.
06 · Recycling

A strategic feedstock, not yet a primary one

Manufacturing scrap supplies most recyclable battery material today because the first large wave of EV packs remains in service. End-of-life volumes rise toward the end of the decade, creating a more dependable stream of lithium, nickel, copper and other materials.

Economics differ by chemistry. Nickel-rich packs offer higher near-term material value, while LFP recycling relies more heavily on efficient logistics, automated disassembly and regulation. Second-life use can extend the value of suitable packs, but testing and warranty costs mean it will complement—not replace—material recovery.

07 · Outlook

Lower costs broaden both markets

GreenScope expects pack costs to trend lower through 2028, with temporary pauses when commodity or trade conditions tighten. Affordable LFP cells will support lower-priced EVs and four-hour stationary systems, while higher-energy chemistries improve through silicon-enhanced anodes, better thermal design and incremental cathode advances.

The most important transition is from a battery market defined by scarcity to one differentiated by execution. Buyers will compare lifetime throughput, safety, traceability and integration—not simply the first quoted price. That discipline should make cost reductions more durable and expand storage’s role across transport and electricity systems.
