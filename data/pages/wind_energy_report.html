Wind Energy Market and Technology Outlook

A maturing onshore sector and a resetting offshore market enter a new phase—defined by taller machines, stricter capital discipline and the value of dependable clean power.

01 · Market outlook

Growth returns with firmer foundations

Wind markets in 2026 are less exuberant and more investable. Developers have repriced risk, turbine suppliers have restored contract discipline and governments are adjusting auctions to acknowledge higher financing and construction costs. The reset has delayed some projects, but it has also produced more credible schedules and clearer responsibilities.

GreenScope models 146 GW of new global wind capacity this year. Onshore installations provide nearly four-fifths of the total, reflecting shorter development cycles and a broad base of mature suppliers. Offshore wind contributes less volume but remains strategically important for densely populated coastal systems seeking large blocks of low-carbon electricity.

02 · Onshore vs offshore

Two markets, one resource

Onshore and offshore wind share core technology but operate as distinct infrastructure classes. Onshore projects offer lower costs and modular delivery. Offshore arrays access steadier resources and larger turbines, while requiring specialised vessels, subsea networks and long-term port capacity.

Neither form is universally preferable. System value depends on site quality, network access and how closely generation aligns with local demand. Port constraints can dominate offshore schedules; community acceptance and transmission routing often set the boundary onshore.

03 · Technology development

Larger rotors, smarter operations

Turbine innovation is becoming more selective. Instead of pursuing size at any cost, manufacturers are standardising platforms and improving reliability. Longer blades increase swept area, advanced controls reduce loads during turbulent conditions and sensor-driven maintenance helps operators intervene before component failures become prolonged outages.

04 · Project economics

Finance becomes part of the technology

Capital costs have stabilised, but borrowing terms remain central to the delivered price of wind energy. Successful projects match auction commitments to realistic procurement schedules, use inflation-linked revenue where appropriate and allocate construction risks to parties able to manage them.

Corporate buyers are also becoming more selective. Power purchase agreements increasingly account for hourly generation profiles and congestion rather than relying on annual energy matching. That change favours well-sited wind, whose evening and winter output can complement daytime solar portfolios.

05 · Supply-chain challenges

Industrial depth sets the pace

Bottlenecks persist in high-voltage equipment, castings, installation vessels and specialised labour. These constraints do not stop global expansion, but they create local sequencing problems and widen the gap between announced and finance-ready projects.

  • Grid equipment: transformers and export cables have the longest procurement horizons.
  • Ports and vessels: offshore markets need coordinated investment before turbine installation begins.
  • Supplier health: better indexation and warranty terms are restoring sustainable margins.
  • Skills: technicians, marine crews and power-system engineers remain in high demand.
06 · Future outlook

A steadier, more valuable build-out

GreenScope expects wind deployment to grow through the late 2020s, led by onshore repowering, new Asian offshore markets and projects with secured network access. Floating wind will advance through demonstration arrays but is unlikely to deliver large volumes before infrastructure and standardisation improve.

The strongest projects will pair high-quality resources with conservative engineering and credible routes to market. Wind’s strategic value grows as power systems seek renewable output beyond the solar day; realising it requires patient capital and coordinated investment in grids, ports and supply chains.
